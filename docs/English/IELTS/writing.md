# IELTS Academic Writing

## At a glance

- **Time:** 60 minutes for two tasks.
- **Task 1:** Describe, summarise, or explain visual information in at least 150 words; allow about 20 minutes.
- **Task 2:** Write an essay of at least 250 words; allow about 40 minutes. Task 2 contributes twice as much as Task 1.
- **Assessment:** Task Achievement (Task 1) / Task Response (Task 2), Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy.

Accuracy, clarity, and control matter more than forcing complicated vocabulary or grammar. See the official [Academic Writing format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing) and [Writing band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf).

## General writing strategy

1. Read the prompt carefully and identify exactly what it asks.
2. Identify the task subtype and every command word or question part.
3. Plan the answer before writing.
4. Select the key features or ideas; do not try to include everything.
5. Organise paragraphs so each has a clear purpose.
6. Support claims with accurate data or explained examples.
7. Leave a few minutes to proofread.

## Writing Task 1

### What is Writing Task 1?

- An Academic report describing, summarising and comparing visual information.
- **At least 150 words · approximately 20 minutes · formal, objective language.**
- Select main features, not every detail. No personal opinions.
- In the exam, write connected prose, not bullets. Plan briefly and leave time to check.

| Task group | Visuals |
| --- | --- |
| Charts & Tables | Line graphs, bar charts, pie charts and tables |
| Processes | Natural or man-made processes; how something works |
| Maps | Geographical changes, comparisons and proposed future developments |

### Structure

Use the same basic structure for all task types; adapt grouping to the visual.

#### Introduction — Paraphrase the question

- Rephrase the task statement using synonyms or a different sentence structure.
- Preserve the meaning: do not force synonyms that change it.
- State what is shown; include dates, locations and units when relevant.

**Useful structures:** The chart illustrates... · The diagram shows... · The maps compare... · The figure provides information about... · The graph depicts changes in...

**Paraphrase:** “The chart shows car sales” → “The chart illustrates the number of cars sold.”

#### Overview — Main features

- Summarise the main picture: usually **2–3 key trends, differences or stages**, not necessarily exactly three.
- Avoid detailed figures, personal opinions and explanations not supported by the visual.
- **Treat a clear overview as essential for a good score.** Do not just repeat the introduction.

**Useful structures:** Overall, it is clear that... · Overall, the most noticeable feature is... · In general, ... whereas... · The most significant change is... · While X increased, Y decreased...

#### Body Paragraphs — Specific details

- Usually **1–2 body paragraphs; two are recommended** for clarity.
- Support the overview with relevant figures or process/map details.
- Group logically; compare directly where relevant rather than describing visuals separately.
- Include important peaks, lows, stages, changes or exceptions. Avoid repetition.

**Useful links:** Looking first at... · Regarding... · By contrast, ... · Meanwhile, ... · Similarly, ... · Compared with/to... · Whereas... · This was followed by... · Subsequently, ... · Finally, ...

| Part | Include |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction | What the visual shows, paraphrased |
| Overview | Main trends, differences, changes or stages |
| Body 1 | First logical group + supporting details |
| Body 2 | Remaining group + supporting details and comparisons |

### Types of Tasks

#### A. Charts & Tables

Describe and compare numerical information, trends, categories or proportions.

##### Key Features — What to look for

- Units and time periods; read titles, axes, headings and legends.
- Overall trends; starting and ending values; forecasts, if provided.
- Highest/lowest values; biggest increases/decreases.
- Similarities, differences, groups and patterns.
- Stable figures, fluctuations and relevant proportions.

Cover every visual, but select details. Check units and totals before comparing; use trend language only when change is shown.

##### Useful Structures — How to describe it

| Function | Reusable patterns |
| --- | --- |
| **Increase** | rose/increased/grew by...; rose/increased to...; showed a steady upward trend; experienced a significant increase; climbed sharply |
| **Decrease** | fell/dropped/declined by...; fell to...; showed a downward trend; experienced a significant decline; decreased dramatically |
| **Stability & Fluctuation** | remained stable at...; remained relatively unchanged; fluctuated considerably; levelled off at...; showed little variation |
| **Peaks & Lows** | reached a peak of...; peaked at...; reached a low of...; the highest/lowest figure was recorded in... |
| **Comparisons** | was considerably/slightly higher/lower than...; was approximately twice as high as...; the gap between X and Y widened/narrowed; while X increased, Y declined |
| **Proportions** | accounted for...; represented...% of the total |

| Pattern | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| increased **BY** | Size of the change | increased by 20 |
| increased **TO** | Final value | increased to 60 |
| increased **FROM X TO Y** | Starting and ending values | increased from 40 to 60 |

- **20% → 30% = +10 percentage points**, or a 50% relative increase.
- Match intensity to the data: *increased sharply*, not “went up a lot”. *Levelled off* means became stable; *fluctuated* means moved up and down repeatedly.
- Match tense to dates: past simple for finished periods; present perfect only for change continuing to now; future forms for forecasts (*is projected to reach...*).
- Use *number* for countable items, *amount* for uncountable quantities; *twice as many/much as* as appropriate. Use *respectively* only when the order is clear.

#### B. Processes

Describe how something works, develops or is produced. If two processes or environments are shown, compare their main differences.

##### Key Features — What to look for

- Starting/ending points, **if any**; linear or cyclical; natural or man-made.
- Main stages and order; sequential or simultaneous actions.
- Inputs, outputs, transformations, repeated stages and loops.
- Differences between diagrams, if more than one is shown.
- For systems or objects: main components, connections and operation.

Follow arrows and labels. Do not assume clear endpoints or invent stages.

##### Useful Structures — How to describe it

| Function | Reusable patterns |
| --- | --- |
| **Sequence** | The process begins with...; Initially, ...; At the next stage, ...; This is followed by...; Subsequently, ...; Once this stage is complete, ...; Finally, ...; The cycle then repeats. |
| **Transformation** | X is converted/transformed into Y; X passes through...; X is transported to...; X undergoes...; X is produced/collected/processed... |
| **Comparison** | Compared with...; Whereas X..., Y...; X is reduced/increased in...; There is a greater/lower level of...; Unlike X, Y...; Both processes involve...; The main difference lies in... |

- Use **present simple** for general natural processes and system operation.
- Use **passive voice** often in manufacturing when the action matters more than the agent; describe actions, not instructions.
- Do not invent causes, consequences, agents or purposes not shown.

#### C. Maps

Describe and compare geographical layouts, developments or changes over time.

##### Key Features — What to look for

- Time periods: past, present or future.
- Main changes; new, removed or replaced features; changes in size or land use.
- Urbanisation/development; residential, agricultural, industrial and commercial areas.
- Unchanged features; major similarities and differences between maps.
- Location and direction: check the compass/key; do not assume the top is north.

Group details by area or type of change. Compare maps directly; do not invent a feature's use or impact.

##### Useful Structures — How to describe it

| Function | Reusable patterns |
| --- | --- |
| **Changes** | X was replaced by Y; X was converted into Y; X was demolished/removed; X was constructed/built; X was expanded/extended; X was relocated; X underwent significant development; The area became more urbanised. |
| **Location** | to the north/south/east/west of...; in the northern/southern part of...; in the centre of...; adjacent to...; opposite...; alongside...; near/next to...; between X and Y... |
| **Comparison** | Previously, the area contained...; By [year], X had been replaced by Y; While X remained unchanged, Y was...; The most noticeable change was...; The area changed from... to...; X remained in the same location. |

**Tenses:** past simple = completed past changes; present simple = current features; future forms/passive = proposals or predictions (*X is planned / will be built*). Use past perfect only to place a past change before another past reference point.

### Final Checklist

- [ ] Introduction paraphrases the task.
- [ ] Clear overview with main features.
- [ ] Relevant details selected and grouped.
- [ ] Comparisons included where appropriate.
- [ ] Correct tense, grammar and units.
- [ ] At least 150 words.
- [ ] Spelling and factual accuracy checked.

## Writing Task 2

<a id="general-overview"></a>

### 1. Overview

- Write an academic essay responding to a point of view, argument or problem.
- **Minimum 250 words · recommended time: 40 minutes.** Use connected prose, not notes or bullets.
- Task 2 carries twice the weight of Task 1: approximately **66.7% of the Writing score**.
- Consider starting with Task 2 to prioritise its weight. This is a strategy, not an IELTS requirement; complete both tasks.
- Use a formal or neutral academic style.

### 2. Marking Criteria

Each criterion contributes **25% of the Task 2 score**. See the official [Writing band descriptors](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf).

| Criterion | What is assessed | Common mistakes to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Task Response (TR) — 25% | Answer every part; give a clear, relevant position where required; develop ideas with explanations and examples. | Irrelevant arguments, incomplete answers, unsupported ideas or an unclear position. |
| Coherence and Cohesion (CC) — 25% | Logical progression; clear paragraphs with central ideas; natural linking and referencing. | Poor paragraph organisation, unclear pronouns, excessive or mechanical linkers. |
| Lexical Resource (LR) — 25% | Vocabulary range, accuracy and precision; appropriate academic words and natural collocations. | Repetition, wrong word choices, spelling/word-formation errors, forced sophisticated vocabulary. |
| Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA) — 25% | Varied sentence structures, including complex sentences; accurate grammar, construction and punctuation. | Frequent errors, uncontrolled complex sentences or incorrect punctuation. |

### 3. Essay Structure

#### Introduction

- Introduce the topic by paraphrasing the question without changing its meaning.
- Give a clear thesis or position where appropriate; avoid lengthy background information.

#### Main Body (2 or more paragraphs)

- Develop **one central idea per paragraph**, with reasoning and examples.
- Adapt the number and purpose of paragraphs to the question; two or three often work well.
- Do not force a balanced for/against structure when a clear opinion is required.

#### Conclusion

- Summarise the main arguments and restate the overall position or answer.
- Do not add new ideas or evidence.

### 4. Essay Plan

#### Step 1 — Analyse the question

- Read carefully; underline instructions: **what exactly must I answer?**
- Identify the topic, limits and important keywords, including any named groups.
- Identify the essay type; turn every command/question into a planning checkpoint.

<a id="question-subtypes"></a>
<a id="opinion-agree-or-disagree"></a>
<a id="discussion-discuss-both-views-and-give-your-opinion"></a>
<a id="advantages-and-disadvantages"></a>
<a id="causes-and-solutions-problems-and-solutions"></a>
<a id="cause-and-effect-reasons-and-consequences"></a>
<a id="two-part-direct-questions"></a>

| Essay Type | Typical Question Language | How to Structure the Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opinion / Agree or Disagree | “To what extent do you agree or disagree?” / “Do you agree or disagree?” | State your position in the introduction; develop supporting arguments in the body; maintain it through the conclusion. Partial agreement needs clear limits. |
| Discussion (Both Views) | “Discuss both views and give your own opinion.” | Explain both views fairly, normally one body paragraph each. Include your opinion when requested, making it clear from the introduction. |
| Advantages and Disadvantages | “What are the advantages and disadvantages?” / “Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?” | Develop both sides. For *outweigh*, state which is stronger and explain why; compare importance, not just the number of points. A plain advantages/disadvantages question does not require that verdict. |
| Problem and Solution | “What problems does this cause?” / “What measures could be taken to solve these problems?” | Explain problems, then relevant solutions; show who could act and how each solution addresses a problem. If asked for causes instead, identify causes, not effects. |
| Cause and Effect | “What are the reasons for this?” / “What effects does this have?” | Explain relevant causes and consequences, usually in separate body paragraphs. Follow the exact question; do not add solutions unless asked. |
| Direct Question / Two-Part Question | “Why is this happening?” / “Is this a positive or negative development?” | Answer every question explicitly, usually one body paragraph per main question. Summarise both answers in the conclusion. |

<a id="positive-or-negative-development"></a>
<a id="hybrid-and-multi-part-prompts"></a>

- A standalone *positive/negative development* prompt is evaluative: state an overall judgement and justify it, not just describe the change.
- These are common preparation labels, not an exhaustive official taxonomy. Questions can overlap or combine types: **the wording takes priority**, as the official [Task 2 prompt guide](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/ielts-writing-task-2-how-to-understand-ielts-question-prompts) explains.
- *Argumentative/persuasive* broadly describe defending a position; *discursive* explores perspectives; *thesis–antithesis* contrasts positions. They are not extra official IELTS categories or compulsory paragraph patterns.

#### Step 2 — Brainstorm ideas

- List possible arguments briefly; select the strongest, most relevant ones.
- Prefer ideas you can explain and support. Do not choose more than you can fully develop.

#### Step 3 — Organise the paragraphs

Plan each body paragraph around:

1. **Point:** one central idea answering the question.
2. **Supporting reason 1:** why the idea is valid.
3. **Supporting reason 2:** a further explanation, mechanism or consequence.
4. **Example or result:** a relevant illustration or outcome.
5. **Link:** connect the argument back to the question.

**PEEL: P — Point → E — Explain → E — Example → L — Link.** This is a flexible framework, not a rigid formula. Two supporting reasons are a planning recommendation, not an IELTS requirement; avoid unrelated ideas or repetitive link sentences.

**Abstract outline:** [Main claim] → [Why it works] → [Further mechanism/consequence] → [Brief illustration] → [How this supports the answer].

#### Step 4 — Write the essay

- Follow the plan and keep the thesis consistent.
- Develop each paragraph fully before introducing another idea.
- Use linking words only for genuine logical relationships; pronouns and clear references also create cohesion.

#### Step 5 — Review and correct

Reserve **about 5 minutes**, or 5–10 minutes if time management allows, within the 40-minute allocation.

- [ ] Have I answered every part of the question?
- [ ] Is my position clear and consistent where required?
- [ ] Does every body paragraph have one central idea?
- [ ] Have I explained and supported my arguments?
- [ ] Is the essay at least 250 words?
- [ ] Have I checked grammar, spelling, articles, verb agreement and punctuation?

### 5. Writing Tips & Tricks

- [ ] Focus: exact question answered; instructions/keywords underlined; ideas brainstormed.
- [ ] Development: strong arguments, not a long list; one idea per paragraph; flexible PEEL; consistent position where required.
- [ ] Language: accuracy before complexity; a controlled mix of simple and complex sentences.
- [ ] Register: avoid contractions; choose natural collocations, not fancy vocabulary; paraphrase without replacing every word.
- [ ] Evidence: use relevant examples from knowledge or experience; do not invent statistics, studies or sources.
- [ ] Delivery: avoid memorised templates and excessive linkers; leave time to review.

There is **no fixed maximum word limit**. Writing far beyond 250 words offers no automatic benefit if it reduces accuracy, relevance or checking time.

### 6. Useful Structures & Linking Words

Choose by function, not variety alone. For sentence connectors, use a full stop or semicolon before them: “Costs rose. However, demand fell.” **However = contrast; therefore = consequence.** Do not join two full sentences with only a comma.

#### A. Adding information

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Also | Adds a related point | Usually before the main verb: “It also saves time.” |
| In addition / Additionally | Adds another relevant point | At the start of a sentence, followed by a comma. |
| Furthermore / Moreover | Adds support that strengthens an argument | When the next point reinforces the same case; use sparingly. |

#### B. Contrast and concession

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| However | Contrasts with the previous statement | Introduce a different or limiting point. |
| Nevertheless | Concedes a point but maintains the argument | “It is costly. Nevertheless, it is necessary.” |
| On the other hand | Presents a contrasting perspective | Only for a genuine alternative, not another supporting point. |
| Whereas | Compares contrasting facts | Join clauses: “X saves time, whereas Y costs less.” |
| Although / Even though | Concedes a fact; *even though* is stronger | **+ clause:** “Although it is costly, it helps.” Do not add *but*. |
| Despite / In spite of | Concedes an obstacle | **+ noun/-ing:** “Despite the cost, it helps.” / “Despite costing more, it helps.” Not *despite of*. |

#### C. Expressing cause

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Because | Gives a direct reason | **+ clause:** “Demand fell because prices rose.” |
| Since / As | Gives a reason, often already understood | + clause; avoid ambiguity with time meanings. |
| Due to / Owing to | Introduces a cause | **+ noun phrase:** “Demand fell due to higher prices.” Not *due to prices rose*. |
| One reason for this is... | Identifies one cause | + noun phrase, or *that + clause*. |

#### D. Expressing results and consequences

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Therefore / Consequently / As a result | Introduces a consequence | After explaining its cause: “Prices rose. Therefore, demand fell.” |
| Thus | Introduces a result or logical inference | A concise, formal alternative; use only when the logic is clear. |
| This can lead to... / This may result in... | Shows a possible consequence | + noun/-ing: “This may result in higher costs.” Avoid claiming certainty without support. |

#### E. Giving examples

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| For example / For instance | Introduces an illustration | Can introduce a full sentence: “For example, buses reduce car use.” |
| Such as | Introduces examples within a phrase | + nouns: “public transport such as buses”. Not *such as buses reduce traffic*. |
| To illustrate this... | Signals a brief illustration | “To illustrate this, consider...” Keep the example relevant. |

#### F. Expressing opinions and arguments

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In my view... / I believe that... | States your own position | When an opinion or judgement is required. |
| I would argue that... | Presents a reasoned claim | Follow with an argument you will support. |
| It could be argued that... | Introduces a possible view | Often for an alternative argument; it does not establish your own position. |
| While I acknowledge that..., I believe... | Concedes a point, then states your position | Follow both parts with clauses; keep the final position clear. |

#### G. Introducing and developing ideas

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One major reason is that... | Introduces a main reason | + clause, often as a topic sentence. |
| Another important factor is... | Adds a distinct factor | + noun phrase, or *that + clause*. |
| This is mainly because... | Explains the previous claim | + clause; make the reference to *this* clear. |
| This suggests that... | Draws a cautious inference | After reasoning or an example, not as proof of certainty. |
| A possible drawback is that... | Introduces a potential disadvantage | + clause, then explain its significance. |

#### H. Concluding

| Expression | Meaning / Function | When to Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In conclusion / To conclude | Signals the final summary | Restate the answer without adding evidence. |
| Overall | Gives a broad summary | Summarise the main judgement, not a minor detail. |
| On balance | Weighs competing considerations | Give the final verdict after considering both sides. |
| Taking these factors into account... | Bases a judgement on earlier points | Use a logical subject: “Taking these factors into account, I favour X.” |

## Language toolkit

### Hedging and cautious claims

Academic writing often needs measured claims. Hedging is not vagueness; it signals the strength and limits of a claim.

| Purpose | Useful forms | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Possibility | may, might, could | “Flexible hours may improve staff retention.” |
| Typical tendency | tends to, is likely to, often | “Long commutes tend to reduce the time available for exercise.” |
| Limited evidence | appears to, suggests, is associated with | “The pattern suggests a link between access and participation.” |
| Scope | in many cases, for some groups, to a certain extent | “In many cases, online services are easier to access.” |

Do not hedge facts shown clearly in Task 1: “The figure fell from 60 to 40” is better than “The figure may have fallen.” In Task 2, avoid *always, never,* and *everyone* unless you can justify an absolute claim.

### Nominalisation

Nominalisation turns an action or quality into a noun. It can make academic prose concise, but too many abstract nouns make sentences heavy.

- **Verb → noun:** “People consume more energy” → “Energy consumption has increased.”
- **Adjective → noun:** “The city became more resilient” → “The city’s resilience improved.”
- **Use with a clear agent or link:** “The expansion of public transport has reduced car use.”

Prefer a direct verb when it is clearer: “The council expanded the service” is often better than “The expansion of the service was undertaken by the council.”

### Useful complex structures

Use a range of structures only when you can control them.

- **Relative clause:** “Cities that invest in reliable public transport can reduce dependence on cars.”
- **Concession:** “Although the measure is costly, it could benefit a large part of the population.”
- **Conditional:** “If employers offered predictable shifts, workers might find childcare easier to arrange.”
- **Cause and result:** “Because the cost is spread over several years, the initial investment is more manageable.”
- **Participle clause:** “Compared with the previous year, the figure was substantially lower.” Use only when the subject logically performs or experiences the implied action.
- **Parallel comparison:** “The policy not only reduces waste but also encourages reuse.” Keep the grammar balanced after *not only…but also*.

### Cohesion without overloading linkers

Use signposting to show a real relationship, not as decoration. Useful functions include:

- **Add:** *also, furthermore, in addition*.
- **Contrast:** *however, whereas, by contrast, although*.
- **Reason/result:** *because, therefore, consequently, as a result*.
- **Example:** *for example, for instance*.
- **Concession:** *admittedly, even though, despite this*.

Avoid beginning every sentence with a connector. Pronouns and repeated key terms can create clearer links than strings of *Moreover* and *Furthermore*.

### Common language errors

- **Countable / uncountable:** *fewer cars* but *less traffic*; *many people* but *much evidence*.
- **Articles:** *the government, a policy, public transport* (usually no article when referring to the system generally).
- **Agreement:** *the number of users is*; *a number of users are*.
- **Plural forms:** *research* is usually uncountable; *evidence* is uncountable; *information* does not take a plural *-s*.
- **Collocations:** *make a decision, pose a risk, conduct research, play a role, address a problem*.
- **Register:** prefer *children* to *kids*, *purchase* only where natural, and *people* rather than inflated substitutes such as *individuals* in every sentence.
- **Precision:** do not use a thesaurus synonym if it changes the meaning or collocation.

## Final proofreading checklist

- [ ] I answered every question and command word.
- [ ] My overview (Task 1) or position/thesis (Task 2) is clear.
- [ ] Each paragraph has one main purpose and progresses logically.
- [ ] Task 1 values, dates, units, comparisons, locations, and sequence are accurate.
- [ ] Task 2 ideas are explained and examples support the point.
- [ ] I used cautious language for uncertain claims and direct language for clear data.
- [ ] I checked verb tense, subject–verb agreement, articles, plurals, punctuation, and spelling.
- [ ] My vocabulary is precise and natural; I did not force memorised phrases.
- [ ] I met the minimum word count and used connected prose.
